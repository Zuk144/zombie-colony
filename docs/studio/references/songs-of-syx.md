# Songs of Syx (Gamatron, early access 2020–)

**Why it's here (owner):** a colony builder that goes from a handful of settlers to an empire
without losing the individual.

**Genre:** city builder plus grand strategy, made by essentially one developer. You go from a
small colony to a metropolis of **tens of thousands of individually simulated citizens,** each
with a race, a religion, fears, and desires, then out to a world map of provinces and battles
of up to 50,000 units.

## How it plays

You pick a race and a starting spot (fertility, sweet water, ore deposits). You build
production chains (farms → mills → bakeries, cotton → weavers → tailors) and housing, then
services (baths, temples, taverns, arenas). Nobles, law, and religion come next. Raids and
battles push you into an army and eventually conquest on the world map. Players sum up the
early game as the **"three Fs": Food, Freeze, Fight.** Roughly half the population farms until
research frees them.

## Systems worth knowing

- **Races with needs:** 6 playable races, each with preferred foods, building materials, climate,
  religion, and attitudes to the other races. Humans want meat and bread in stone buildings;
  Cretonians want vegetables and fruit in wood. **What people want shapes what you build.**
- **Happiness drives growth.** Happiness pulls immigrants in and feeds loyalty. Its inputs are
  population, access, services, environment, religion, occupation, and government. Unhappy
  citizens riot, and a **revolt spawns them as an enemy division that marches on your throne
  room.**
- **Decrees as levers:** handouts (cost money, brief joy), a day off (joy, lost production),
  overtime (production, misery), prosecution (fear). Short-term trades with visible costs.
- **Classes:** plebs, nobles (boost production and want luxury), and slaves (cheap, risk
  revolt). Each class is a different relationship with the city.
- **Terraforming water:** digging canals raises fertility nearby, but each map has a fixed
  **water table,** so to place water here you must remove it there. A finite resource you
  *arrange*, not just spend.
- **Clothing vs winter:** without clothes, citizens freeze. Cotton → fabric → clothes is a
  survival chain, not a luxury.
- **A living world:** weather, droughts, pestilence, migrations. "Small events can spiral into
  the collapse of kingdoms."
- **Raids:** you can fight them or pay them off, if your economy exports enough.

## Art and UX

- **Tiny, crisp, minimal pixel art at enormous scale.** You zoom from one citizen carrying bread
  to a city of 20,000 and it stays legible. Scale itself is the spectacle.
- Dense but functional panels, overlays (fertility, base fertility, access), and an in-game
  wiki. Heavy on data; approachable only once learned.
- An orchestral soundtrack and a low-fantasy tone that's grounded, not whimsical.

## Why it's fun

1. **Scale with individuals.** A metropolis where every dot has a life. Zooming in on one
   citizen in a city of thousands is magic.
2. **Needs create the city's shape.** Different races make different cities.
3. **Visible, readable collapse:** a riot is an army marching through your streets, not a
   number going down.
4. **Clear early priorities** (Food, Freeze, Fight) that turn into strategic depth later.

## What to take (principles)

- **Unhappiness becomes a physical event.** A revolt marching on the throne room is our mental
  break made visible. A mob of survivors leaving with supplies would be a colony-scale version.
- **Decrees = policies with visible costs.** "Double shifts" (more work, more stress), "Rations"
  (food saved, mood down), "Lights out" (quieter Din, and scared people). These fit indirect
  control perfectly and would be ours.
- **A finite resource you rearrange** (the water table). For us this could be how many squares
  of fence one power grid can electrify, or a noise budget.
- **Survival chains before luxury chains:** Food, Freeze, Fight is exactly our early game (we
  have winter, clothing isn't built yet, and zombies).
- **Scale as a late-game payoff:** a colony that grows from 3 to 30, still with names.

## What to avoid

- **Spreadsheet-heavy panels.** Syx's UI is a mouse-and-monitor data wall.
- **The grand-strategy layer as a second game.** Our world map (expeditions) must stay light.
- **Scale that erases people.** Past about 30 survivors, our stories die. Cap growth in a way
  that makes sense for the fiction.

## How it maps to us

Syx is what happens when the colony keeps growing, and it shows how to keep a big population
legible. Our key takeaways are **policies (decrees) as indirect control** and **riots as
visible events.** Both let the player shape a colony without touching a person.
