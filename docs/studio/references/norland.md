# Norland (Long Jaunt / Hooded Horse; early access July 2024)

**Why it's here (owner):** a newer colony sim that pushes indirect control and social drama in
a different direction than RimWorld.

**Genre:** a medieval kingdom sim and story generator. Reviewers pitch it as "RimWorld's
absurdity meets Crusader Kings' intrigue." You're a **noble family** ruling a settlement in a
fallen empire. You don't control peasants; you manage through your nobles. About 82% positive
on Steam in early access. The game is still changing (big reworks of its core mechanics have landed since
launch), so treat details as a snapshot. There's less wiki documentation than for the other
games here.

## How it plays

Your family members are the only people who can lead: they run buildings, set work and
priorities, lead militias, trade, and pursue their own ambitions (romance, status symbols,
piety). Peasants work, drink, pray, gossip, commit crimes, and revolt. Around you are rival
lords, holy caravans, and an "Unholy Horde" beyond the borders.

## Systems worth knowing

- **Control through a class of people.** Only nobles can manage, so **your leaders are the
  scarce resource.** Losing a noble means a building or army without a manager.
- **Peasant needs are simple and social:** food, wages, and **beer.** Loyalist peasants just want
  enough money for food and beer; devout ones (the Church of Saint Sophia) care about a noble's
  piety. **Different groups want different things from the same rulers.**
- **An economy with wages and prices:** you pay wages and set prices on the domestic market, so
  peasants are both workers and customers.
- **Crime and justice:** unhappy peasants become criminals (theft and worse). You can patrol,
  punish, execute, or enslave, and slaves may escape. Criminals can be *used*: a cutthroat can be
  hired to kill a rival lord.
- **Nobles have their own agendas:** romance, affairs, traits formed by lifestyle ("didn't see
  enough action"), status objects (holy rings), betrayal, secret murders, and succession
  struggles. A common complaint: nobles chase their desires instead of doing what the colony
  needs.
- **Religion and worldview:** faiths and cultural factions affect loyalty and migration. Temples
  can make people fanatical enough to stay.
- **War:** raise militias from peasants, limited by equipment. Battles can be manual or
  auto-resolved; reviewers call manual control fiddly.

## Art and UX

- A cozy, illustrated medieval look with small figures and warm colors, which clashes (on
  purpose, and comically) with the brutality of what happens.
- UI complaints: tiny resource icons and dense panels.

## Why it's fun

1. **The comedy of power:** you're responsible for people who won't cooperate, including your
   own family.
2. **Class makes drama:** the same event means different things to nobles, loyalists, and the
   devout.
3. **Crime as an ecosystem,** not just a penalty: it's a tool, a threat, and a story.
4. **Stories with motives:** a murder has a *reason* (ambition, jealousy, faith).

## What to take (principles)

- **Leaders as the scarce resource.** An indirect-control twist: what if some tasks (running an
  expedition, managing the radio tower) need a survivor with a Leader trait, and losing them
  matters? It keeps control indirect while making certain people precious.
- **Groups with different wants from the same colony:** e.g. "the faithful," "the pragmatists,"
  and "the newcomers who just want food and safety." That leads to choice events where pleasing
  one group upsets another.
- **Small pleasures as a need:** beer, cigarettes, coffee. In a zombie world, **luxuries are a
  scavenging goal** and a mood lever ("the last jar of coffee").
- **Crime and desperation:** in a starving colony, someone steals food, and you decide how to
  respond (a policy, not an order).

## What to avoid

- **Heavy politics and a wage economy.** A colony of 3–20 survivors doesn't need markets; it
  risks turning into a medieval sim with zombies.
- **People who ignore survival for their own agenda to the point of frustration.** Quirks
  should create stories, not paralysis.
- **Tiny UI.** Touch makes this worse; everything must meet 44 pt.

## How it maps to us

Norland shows that indirect control can go further than priorities: you can **govern** instead
of manage. The pieces worth borrowing are key people (a Leader or Doctor as a precious role),
**factions inside the colony,** and luxuries as morale. We keep it survival-sized: no throne,
just a camp that has to decide what kind of people it is.
