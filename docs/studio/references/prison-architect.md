# Prison Architect (Introversion Software, 2015; now Paradox)

**Why it's here (owner):** one of the five named influences in the pitch. It's **the look**:
clean top-down art where everything is readable, and a management sim built on schedules and
rooms.

**Genre:** a top-down prison-management sim. You build and run a prison: foundations, walls,
rooms, utilities, and staff. Hundreds of prisoners live by a daily **regime** (schedule) you
set. You never command a prisoner, only the conditions they live in. Order is the goal; riots,
escapes, and contraband are the threat.

## How it plays

You take government grants and prisoner-intake payments, build cell blocks, a kitchen, canteen,
showers, and yard, then hire cooks, guards, workmen, and janitors. You research the Bureaucracy
tree (warden, psychologist, security chief), which unlocks reports, programs, and policies.
Prisoners arrive in batches with reputations and needs; you keep them fed, clean, busy, and
afraid enough not to riot.

## Systems worth knowing

- **Rooms are defined by what's in them:** a canteen is a zoned area with tables, benches, and a
  serving table; a cell is a bed plus a toilet at minimum size. Build first, then designate.
- **Needs (about 15):** bladder, sleep, food, hygiene, clothing, comfort, exercise, **safety**,
  **freedom**, **family**, recreation, environment, privacy, literacy, and addictions. Status
  runs satisfied → medium → high → critical, and unmet needs lead to complaints, fights, and
  riots. Not every need leads to violence; Clothing and Comfort just grumble.
- **The regime** is an hourly schedule: Lockup, Sleep, Eat, Yard, Shower, Free Time, Work. It
  confines prisoners to places; it doesn't force actions. Cooks start 4 hours before Eat.
  Unscheduled blocks lead to boredom and vandalism. Prisoners can **demand** less work or more
  free time as events, and refusing costs you.
- **You don't know what they need without the right staff:** a psychologist reveals needs.
  **Information is something you unlock.**
- **Contraband:** tools, weapons, and drugs get smuggled or stolen (knives from the kitchen), which
  drives searches, dogs, metal detectors, and informants. Escape tunnels get dug during sleep.
- **Danger and suppression:** more guards lower danger but raise suppression, and suppression
  pushes freedom needs up. Control has a cost.
- **Utilities:** power (a capacitor and wiring) and water (pipes) as buildable infrastructure.
- **Reports:** needs, finance, danger, and regime, as big clear charts.

## Art and UX (the part we inherit)

- **Flat, top-down, cartoon-clean art:** simple shapes, strong outlines, bold solid colors, and
  people as round heads on simple bodies, **readable at every zoom.** It looks almost like a
  diagram of a prison, and that's the point.
- **Walls, floors, and objects are graphic, not realistic:** tiles, benches, and toilets as
  icons-in-place. You can understand the whole building at a glance.
- **Speech-balloon icons** over people show what they want (a food icon, a toilet icon), and red
  balloons mean it's serious.
- **Planning mode:** draw blueprints on the ground before you commit money, then build.
- Everything is built by visible workmen hauling materials, so construction is a show.

## Why it's fun

1. **Order vs chaos:** a well-oiled prison ticking along, then the tension of the riot you
   caused by cutting yard time.
2. **Control has costs:** every lever (guards, lockup, searches) fixes one problem and feeds
   another (freedom, suppression).
3. **Schedules as gameplay:** designing a day is a genuinely satisfying puzzle.
4. **Readable density:** hundreds of people, and you can still tell what each one wants.

## What to take (principles)

- **The art direction.** Our vector style *is* this lineage: flat shapes, bold outlines,
  diagram-clarity, and people you can read at any zoom. Keep it clean; tone comes from light and
  color (our night lighting), not grime.
- **Speech balloons for needs** as the main touch-friendly way to show what someone wants.
- **Schedules that confine, not force.** Our schedule and guard shifts already work this way;
  lean in (curfew, lockdown).
- **Control creates its own pressure:** a lockdown keeps people safe but raises cabin fever,
  and a curfew keeps the Din down but hurts morale. That's indirect control with real costs.
- **Information as a reward:** a Doctor reveals infection progress, and a scout reveals horde
  size. You learn more by investing in people.
- **Demands as events:** survivors can ask for a day off or better food, and refusing has a cost.
  That's a great choice-event seed.

## What to avoid

- **The money and grant economy.** Survival isn't funded.
- **Hundreds of people.** Our scale is personal (3–30).
- **Utility pipes and wiring as a separate layer.** We deliberately use poles-with-reach instead.

## How it maps to us

Prison Architect is our visual north star and our model for **indirect control through
schedules and rooms.** The mirror image is thematic: they keep people *in*, we keep the dead
*out*. The secure yard is a prison turned inside out. Borrowing their readability (balloons,
status icons, planning mode) is the fastest way to make a complex sim work on a tablet.
